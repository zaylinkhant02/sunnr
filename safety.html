What Can We Do for Heat Waves?

Extreme temperature spikes can endanger health and community well-being. Practicing simple water conservation, staying hydrated, and checking on vulnerable neighbors keeps everyone safe.

Stay Hydrated

Drink plenty of water consistently throughout the day, even before you feel thirsty, to prevent heat exhaustion.

Keep Indoors Cool

Close curtains during peak daylight hours to block direct sunlight and reduce indoor heat buildup.

Check Your Community

Keep an eye on elderly relatives, pets, and neighbors who may need assistance or access to cool water during severe heat.

Heatwave Safety Prevention Guide

General actions during heatwaves

Simple precautions can make a big difference. Follow these practical steps to protect yourself, your family, and your community during extreme heat.

01 / Exposure Prevention

Stay away from the heat

01 — Protect Yourself

Avoid peak heat hours

Stay indoors

Stay inside during the hottest times of the day. If you must go outside, remain in shaded areas.

Limit physical activity

Avoid strenuous exercise. If necessary, schedule outdoor activities during cooler morning hours, preferably 04:00–07:00.

Wear sun protection

Wear a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, and lightweight clothing.

Never leave anyone in parked vehicles

Never leave children, vulnerable people, or pets inside parked vehicles, even briefly.

02 / Personal Care

Keep your body cool

01 / Hydration

Hydrate regularly

Drink water throughout the day, even when you are not thirsty. Avoid alcohol and excessive caffeine.

02 / Clothing

Dress appropriately

Choose loose-fitting, lightweight clothing and breathable bed linens. Avoid unnecessary cushions that trap heat.

03 / Body Cooling

Use cooling methods

Take cool showers or baths, spray your skin with cool water, or apply damp towels to your body.

03 / Home Protection

Make your home a cool zone

Indoor Temperature Targets
32° Daytime
24° Nighttime

Keep indoor temperatures below these levels, particularly for vulnerable people.

01

Manage windows and sunlight

Close windows, curtains, and blinds during the day. Open windows after dark when outdoor air becomes cooler.

02

Move to cool areas

Rest in the coolest room of your home. If your home remains hot, spend 2–3 hours in an air-conditioned public building.

03

Use appliances wisely

Turn off non-essential electronics. Use air conditioning at a comfortable setting, such as 27°C, with a fan if appropriate.

04

Use fans carefully

Fans may be ineffective or unsafe in very hot conditions. Follow local health advice and prioritize a cooler environment.

04 / Community Care

Look after each other

Extra Care for Vulnerable Groups

Check on the people who need it most.

Heatwaves can be especially dangerous for people who are more vulnerable to high temperatures. Maintain regular contact and make sure they have access to drinking water and cool spaces.

  • Infants and young children
  • Older adults, especially over 65
  • People living alone
  • People with disabilities
  • People with chronic heart, lung, or kidney conditions

Protect children: Never cover a stroller with dry blankets or fabric that traps heat. Keep children shaded, dress them lightly, and ensure good airflow.

05 / Recognize and Respond

Know the signs & Act fast

Recognizing heat-related illnesses early can prevent serious complications and save lives.

Warning Signs

Heat cramps & exhaustion

Watch for early symptoms of heat-related illness.

  • Muscle cramps or spasms
  • Dizziness or headache
  • Heavy sweating and weakness
  • Nausea or unusual tiredness
  • Signs of dehydration
What to Do

Move to a cool place, loosen unnecessary clothing, cool the body with water or wet towels, and sip water or an oral rehydration solution if fully alert and able to swallow.

Seek medical advice if symptoms worsen, persist, or do not improve promptly.
Medical Emergency

Heatstroke

Heatstroke is life-threatening and requires immediate emergency medical attention.

  • Confusion or unusual behaviour
  • Fainting or loss of consciousness
  • Seizures
  • Very high body temperature
  • Hot skin, with or without sweating
Immediate Response

Call emergency services immediately. Move the person to a cool place, remove unnecessary clothing, and rapidly cool them using cold water, wet towels, or ice packs while help is coming.

If unconscious but breathing, place them on their side. Do not give food, drinks, aspirin, or paracetamol. Stay with them and monitor their condition.
Get Instant Help

Suspected heatstroke is a medical emergency. Start cooling immediately while waiting for professional help.

Climate Knowledge / Fact VS Fiction

Separating heatwave myths from facts.

Not everything we hear about extreme heat is true. Misconceptions about climate change can create confusion, delay action, and put communities at risk.

Explore 13 common myths and discover what scientific evidence actually tells us about heatwaves.

01 / Understanding Our Climate

Recognize the facts behind rising temperatures

Know the Difference / 01—13

What people believe vs. what science says

Select each misconception to uncover the facts and understand the science behind extreme heat.

The Fact

Weather has always changed, but today's heat extremes are occurring in a warmer climate. Rising global temperatures increase the likelihood and intensity of extreme heat events. Many of the world's warmest years have occurred in the last decade.

The Fact

There is overwhelming scientific agreement that human activities are driving current global warming. Burning fossil fuels releases greenhouse gases, including carbon dioxide, which trap heat in the atmosphere. Scientists continue researching future impacts, but the main cause of current warming is well established.

The Fact

Natural climate patterns, such as El Niño, influence temperatures and rainfall. However, they cannot explain the long-term rise in global temperatures. Human-caused climate change amplifies the effects of natural weather patterns, making many heatwaves more intense.

The Fact

Changes in Earth's orbit influence climate over thousands of years. They cannot explain the rapid warming observed since the Industrial Revolution. Today's temperature increase closely matches the rise in greenhouse gases from human activities, particularly the burning of coal, oil, and gas.

The Fact

Extreme heat has occurred throughout history. However, today's heatwaves happen against a significantly warmer global background temperature. This makes many extreme heat events more likely and more severe than they would have been in a cooler climate.

The Fact

Climate change does not create every individual weather event, but scientists can measure its influence. Climate attribution studies compare real-world events with simulated climates to determine how human-caused warming changes the likelihood and intensity of heatwaves.

The Fact

Instrumental temperature records extend back more than 150 years. Scientists also study ice cores, tree rings, ocean sediments, and other natural climate evidence to understand Earth's climate over much longer periods. These records show that current warming is exceptionally rapid in the context of recent geological history.

The Fact

While some people enjoy warm weather, extreme heat can create serious health risks. Older adults, young children, outdoor workers, and people with existing health conditions are especially vulnerable. Heat exposure can cause dehydration, heat exhaustion, and life-threatening heatstroke.

The Fact

Adaptation depends on infrastructure, resources, housing, and access to cooling. Countries with warmer climates may have heat-adapted buildings and lifestyles, but not everyone has equal protection. Preparing for extreme heat requires better urban planning, cooling facilities, and public awareness.

The Fact

Heat warnings are designed to protect lives, not create panic. Early alerts give people time to prepare, stay hydrated, avoid dangerous outdoor activities, and check on vulnerable individuals. Public warnings are an important part of heatwave preparedness.

The Fact

Carbon dioxide can support plant growth under certain conditions, but higher concentrations do not guarantee better harvests. Extreme heat, drought, water shortages, and changing rainfall patterns can damage crops and reduce food quality and production.

The Fact

Human activities have significantly changed atmospheric chemistry, land use, and global temperatures. The burning of fossil fuels and large-scale environmental changes demonstrate the scale of human influence. People can also help address the problem through cleaner energy, reduced emissions, and environmental protection.

The Fact

Climate change is a serious challenge, but future warming can still be limited. Every reduction in greenhouse gas emissions matters. Moving towards renewable energy, reducing fossil fuel dependence, and protecting natural environments can reduce future climate risks.

Knowledge is the first step towards preparedness. Understanding the science helps us make informed decisions.

Be Informed / Be Prepared

Facts matter in a changing climate

Heatwaves are more than just hotter versions of ordinary summers. They are part of a wider climate shift influenced by rising greenhouse gas emissions.

Understanding the science helps communities prepare, protect vulnerable people, and make better decisions. While the challenge is serious, the choices we make today can still shape a safer future.

03 / Separating Facts from Fiction

Common Solar Energy Misconceptions

The Fact

Solar panels generate electricity from sunlight rather than heat. Although strong sunshine can increase electricity generation, very high panel temperatures can slightly reduce their efficiency

This means a hot, sunny day can still produce substantial electricity, even when individual panels operate less efficiently

The Fact

Heatwaves do not automatically cause solar panels to perform poorly. Longer daylight hours and increased solar radiation can support high total daily electricity output

While extreme temperatures can lower conversion efficiency slightly, solar systems can continue generating substantial electricity when properly designed and maintained

The Fact

Solar irradiance and panel efficiency are two different things. Solar irradiance determines how much sunlight energy reaches the panels, while efficiency determines how effectively that energy becomes electricity

A cooler sunny day may provide better efficiency per panel, while a longer, brighter summer day may still generate more electricity overall

Understanding how sunlight, temperature and system design work together helps us make better use of solar energy during extreme weather
